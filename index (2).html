01Abstract

This project presents a pick and place maglev robotic arm that integrates planar magnetic levitation technology with conventional mechanical structures, providing 6 degrees of freedom (6 DOF). It addresses three major problems: frictional heating and thermal drift, frictional emissions, and particulate contamination. In conventional mechanical arms, contact between moving components generates friction that can cause heat accumulation, thermal deformation, positioning errors and wear particles. To overcome this, our system provides contactless motion in the X and Y axes, which reduces friction at the positioning stage, while keeping mechanical support on the Z axis for structural stability. A smart controller coordinates the movements between the robotic arm and the components on the wire. The prototype was evaluated on levitation stability, positioning accuracy, thermal behavior and particulate generation. The outcomes show that magnetic levitation minimized friction-related heating and particle emissions, while improving positioning reliability and suitability for applications that need precise motion and reduced contamination. The project aims to demonstrate the feasibility of combining planar magnetic levitation with robotic manipulation as a way to reduce mechanical contact and friction in robotic positioning systems.

02Introduction

This project presents a pick and place maglev robotic arm that integrates planar magnetic levitation with conventional mechanical structures, providing 6 DOF. It addresses three problems: frictional heating and thermal drift, frictional emissions, and particulate contamination. Conventional solutions rely on lubrication, specialized low-friction materials and periodic maintenance to limit friction-related heating, thermal drift and particle generation, which lead to a loss of about 1 trillion to 1.5 trillion dollars due to mechanical friction and component wear. However, these approaches still depend on physical contact between moving surfaces, leaving friction and wear as persistent sources of thermal instability and contamination.

To overcome this, our system provides contactless motion in the X and Y axes, reducing friction at the positioning stage while keeping mechanical support on the Z axis for stability. A smart controller coordinates the robotic arm and the components on the wire. The prototype was evaluated on levitation stability, positioning accuracy, thermal behavior and particulate generation, and the outcomes show reduced friction-related heating and particle emissions with improved positioning reliability. The project aims to demonstrate the feasibility of combining planar magnetic levitation with robotic manipulation.

03Hypothesis

Integrating a maglev planar motor into a 6 DOF pick-and-place arm significantly reduces friction, thermal drift and wear-generated particulate emissions during X-Y positioning, compared with other conventional mechanical arms driven by mechanical motors. By replacing physical contact with contactless electromagnetic motion on the X and Y plane at the base, the proposed system is expected to reduce friction between components and wires, reduce thermally induced errors and minimize particle generation, while keeping the functionality required for pick-and-place operations.

05Procedures and Building

1

Stationary Phase

16 concentric copper coils on a rigid, non-magnetic acrylic board in a precise 4x4 grid surrounding a soft metallic core, forming the inverted ceiling plane. Analog Hall Effect sensors inside the stator grid monitor field intensity and distance, and each coil has its own MOSFET powered from a stable 12V DC supply.

2

Moving Phase

Permanent magnets attached in a Halbach array configuration, which ensures the distribution of magnetic flux, to the top surface of the floating platform. The platform is made of aluminum and carries the robotic hand.

3

Software and Control

The microcontroller (e.g. Arduino IDE) reads the Hall sensors, runs a PID algorithm on the error between desired and actual air gap, and adjusts the PWM signal of each MOSFET in real time to modulate every coil.

Hardware steps

  • Step 1: Support 16 concentric copper coils on a rigid, non-magnetic acrylic board in a precise 4x4 grid surrounding a soft metallic core to form the inverted ceiling plane. The wires are coiled in the form of two squares and connected together, extending only two terminals.
  • Step 2: Add analog Hall Effect sensors within the stator grid to continuously monitor the magnetic field intensity and distance.
  • Step 3: Connect the two terminals of each square to a separate MOSFET transistor for independent control. Connect the power channels of these MOSFETs to a stable 12V DC power supply.
  • Step 4: Connect the analog Hall Effect sensors within the stator medium.
  • Step 5: Connect the signal channel of each MOSFET to the microcontroller, providing it with signals.
  • Moving part: Attach the permanent magnets in a Halbach array configuration, ensuring the distribution of the magnetic flux, to the top surface of the aluminum floating platform.

Software steps

  • Step 6: Program the microcontroller (e.g. using Arduino IDE) to continuously read the analog data from the Hall Effect sensors, establishing the sensory input for the closed-loop system.
  • Step 7: Implement a PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) control algorithm that calculates the exact error between the desired air gap (e.g. 1 mm) and the actual distance measured by the sensors.
  • Step 8: Program the microcontroller to dynamically adjust the Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) signals sent to each specific MOSFET. This modulates the electromagnetic strength of individual coils in real time, preventing the platform from falling or sticking to the stator, and enabling controlled directional movement.

06Test Plan

Axis 1

Frictional Heating and Thermal Drift

Evaluates the thermal efficiency of the zero-contact design. We measure the rate of temperature increase at the interaction plane (the air gap interface) over a continuous operational period. Thermal sensors log the peak temperature and thermal drift, which are compared directly against the baseline heat generated by mechanical friction in a conventional motor running under identical loads and durations.

Axis 2

Wear and Particulate Contamination

Validates suitability for clean-room environments by testing frictional emissions. The operational environment is isolated to observe, collect and quantify the mass of micro-fractures and wear debris produced during movement. The particulate mass from the Maglev system, measured with precision scales, is compared with the average fragment volume emitted by the grinding gears and bearings of a conventional robotic arm.

Axis 3

Levitation Stability and Precision

Builds on the closed-loop control methodology to verify dynamic stability. The air gap distance is continuously logged through the sensor feedback to track any microscopic deviations, vibrations or positional drift. This ensures the automated feedback loop maintains the target air gap without physical contact or system failure.

08Analysis

Heating and thermal drift

The maglev stage warmed by about 3°C over 30 minutes versus 8°C for the conventional stage. The contactless X and Y planar design generates no friction heat because the platform floats above the stator grid. Less heating means less thermal expansion, so thermal error stays near 20 µm instead of 53 µm (about 62% lower). The remaining heat comes from the electrical side: the 16 coils carrying current from the 12 V supply and the MOSFETs switching the PWM signals. That is why the rise is not zero, and if the temperature rises above these results, the coils and MOSFETs are the expected cause.

Wear and particles

The maglev stage raised the particle count in each size by only about 5%, while the conventional stage raised them by around 75% to 200%. The increase is greatest at 5 and 1 µm because larger particles mostly come from guides, bearings and lubricants. Our platform has none of these, so there is no surface to wear. The small residual increase comes from parts that still move or touch, such as the robotic hand, the wires and the Z-axis support. Compared against the ISO 14644-1 limits and the cleanroom class, the room stays clean.

Stability and precision

The prototype holds position within 0.08 mm (σ) while levitated and reaches targets with an RMS error of about 0.36 mm. These values are about five orders of magnitude larger than published research because of sensing and control: published stages use laser interferometers with sub-nanometer resolution, while we use analog Hall Effect sensors limited by noise and by how the field changes with distance. The PID loop can only correct what the sensor sees, so the sensor sets the floor for every number. The 1.5 s settling time depends on PID gains and PWM update rate, and better tuning could shorten it. The repeatability of about 0.14 mm shows that without friction or backlash the stage returns to the same point with a small spread.

Overall, the three results support our hypothesis: integrating a maglev planar motor into a pick-and-place 6 DOF robotic arm significantly reduces friction, thermal drift and wear-generated particulate emissions during X-Y positioning compared with conventional arms driven by mechanical motors. Replacing physical contact with contactless electromagnetic motion on the base reduces friction between components and wires, reduces thermally induced errors and minimizes particle generation, while keeping the functionality required for pick-and-place operations.

09Real-World Significance and Applications

These results matter beyond the lab because friction-induced heat and wear particles are the main limits in semiconductor wafer handling, pharmaceutical cleanrooms and precision manufacturing, where a single particle or a few micrometers of drift can ruin a product. Replacing sliding contact with magnetic levitation removes both sources at the positioning stage, which can reduce maintenance, lubricant use and product rejects. The prototype’s precision is far below that of published nanometer-level maglev stages, but the trend it demonstrates shows that contactless positioning can be combined with pick-and-place manipulation.

10Conclusion

This project showed that integrating planar magnetic levitation into a pick-and-place robotic arm can reduce friction-related problems. In the X-Y plane, contactless motion kept the stage temperature rise to about 3 °C over 30 minutes, compared with 8 °C for a conventional stage, which cut thermal-expansion error from about 53 µm to 20 µm. Airborne particle counts stayed within about 5% of the room baseline, while the conventional stage raised them by 75% to 200%. These results answer our research question and support our hypothesis, because removing sliding contact removed the main sources of heat and wear particles at the positioning stage. The prototype held its position within 0.08 mm (σ) and reached targets with an RMS error of about 0.36 mm. This precision is limited by the Hall-effect sensors and PID tuning, so better sensing would improve it. The approach could be applied in semiconductor wafer handling, pharmaceutical cleanrooms, and laboratory automation.

11Future Recommendations

Z

Z-axis motion using a maglev rod

Add vertical movement to the planar platform by mounting a rod that moves up and down through magnetic levitation. This gives the arm full access to the Z-axis, so it can reach objects at different heights without any mechanical contact or cables.

+

Multiple arms on the same planar platform

Mount more than one robotic arm on the same levitated platform. This allows the system to handle several tasks at the same time, such as holding an object with one arm while another works on it, making the platform fully functional.

AI

Computer vision for autonomous operation

Integrate a camera and computer vision algorithms so the arm can automatically detect objects, locate them, and move toward them on its own, without a human controlling each movement.
